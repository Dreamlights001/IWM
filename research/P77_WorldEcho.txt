                                             Abstract
                                             Action-conditioned world models increasingly serve as learned simulators for robotic pol-
                                             icy improvement, yet their reliability rests on an unverified assumption: generated futures
                                             faithfully reflect the consequences of arbitrary valid actions. Systematic evaluation against
                                             action-specific ground truth remains limited beyond expert demonstrations. To address this
                                             gap, we introduce WorldEcho, which evaluates action following over a broader action distri-
                                             bution through visual integrity and SE(3) end-effector trajectory alignment. Our diagnosis
                                             shows that expert-trained models perform better on demonstrated actions but struggle with
                                             off-expert trajectories, producing motion inconsistent with the commanded actions or vi-
                                             sually invalid rollouts. To mitigate these failures, we propose WorldSync, which broadens
                                             action coverage, grounds intermediate video representations in action-induced robot dynam-
                                             ics through an Action-Forcing Expert, and aligns predicted and ground-truth changes under
                                             action interventions. Experiments show that WorldSync reduces integrity-gated error on
                                             RoboTwin and, when used for iterative policy improvement, yields higher success rates in
                                             simulation and on real robots.

measure whether generated futures faithfully reflect the queried actions. Synthetic experience may
benefit policy learning through visual augmentation even when its action responses are inaccurate.
Direct evaluation must therefore compare model predictions with the consequences of executing the
same actions from the same initial state. Learned policies may deviate from expert behavior during
evaluation and improvement, so these comparisons must also cover off-expert actions (Ross et al.,
2011; Yu et al., 2026; Guo et al., 2026a). Yet existing benchmarks mainly assess perceptual and
semantic quality, reference similarity, or downstream executability (Hu et al., 2025; Shang et al.,
2026a; Jiang et al., 2026a). Although recent studies probe out-of-distribution or failure-inducing
actions (Quevedo et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026a), systematic comparisons against action-specific
ground truth across broad numerical action queries remain limited.
To address this gap, WorldEcho evaluates action following on demonstrated queries and four com-
plementary off-expert categories (Figure 1). Each query is replayed in RoboTwin to obtain its
ground-truth future, including unsuccessful outcomes. We assess generated rollouts through visual
integrity and SE(3) alignment between generated and reference end-effector trajectories. Relative
to demonstrated queries, off-expert queries increase integrity-gated error across six expert-trained
models (Figure 5), with both visually plausible action mismatches and visual degradation (Figure 2).
These findings motivate WorldSync, which combines distributional coverage, representational
grounding, and intervention-effect alignment. Simulated expert and off-expert trajectories broaden
action support, while a small amount of target-domain real-world data preserves visual fidelity. Be-
cause coverage alone does not ground intermediate video representations in action consequences, an
Action-Forcing Expert (AFE) predicts future end-effector state changes. AFE supervises individual
rollouts but does not explicitly constrain changes across actions. Intervention-Effect (IE) super-
vision therefore aligns predicted and ground-truth changes across paired trajectories with shared
observations and different actions.
On RoboTwin (Chen et al., 2026b), WorldSync with and without additional robotic pretraining
achieves the two lowest integrity-gated error point estimates among evaluated configurations (Ta-
ble 1). When used for policy improvement, it achieves higher success rates than expert-trained
CtrlWorld in simulation and across four real-robot tasks on three platforms (Figure 6).
Our contributions are as follows. (1) WorldEcho measures visual integrity and action-specific
SE(3) trajectory alignment across demonstrated and off-expert queries. (2) Our evaluation charac-
terizes off-expert failures across six world models, distinguishing action-inconsistent motion from

visual degradation. (3) WorldSync improves action following through expanded coverage, AFE, and
IE supervision, with downstream evaluation of policy improvement.

2     R ELATED W ORK
2.1   ACTION -C ONDITIONED ROBOTIC W ORLD M ODELS

AC-WMs advance action control through architecture (Zhu et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2026b; Zheng
et al., 2026), broader pretraining (Gao et al., 2026; Wu & Gao, 2026; Huang et al., 2026b), and
spatially grounded representations (Wu & Gao, 2026; Chen et al., 2026c;a; Yang et al., 2026b). Per-
turbations and counterfactual behaviors extend beyond standard replay, but provide mostly indirect
or coarse-grained evidence of action following rather than ground-truth motion for each command
(Zhu et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2026; Huang et al., 2026b; Feingold et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026a).

2.2   W ORLD M ODELS FOR P OLICY E VALUATION AND I MPROVEMENT

AC-WMs support policy evaluation (Quevedo et al., 2026; Li et al., 2025b; Jeon et al., 2026; Ma
et al., 2026) and improvement using synthetic experience (Yu et al., 2026; Xiao et al., 2026; Li et al.,
2025a; Jiang et al., 2026b). To handle policy queries beyond expert-dominated data, systems use
exploratory or corrective interactions (Yin et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026b; Xiao et al., 2026), generation
filtering, or joint policy and world-model updates (Jiang et al., 2026b; Guo et al., 2026a; Liu et al.,
2026). These downstream gains motivate direct evaluation of the queried actions’ consequences.

2.3   ROBOTIC W ORLD M ODEL E VALUATION

Benchmarks assess motion and physical consistency (Hu et al., 2025; Shang et al., 2026a;b), or
recover actions to test executability (Jiang et al., 2026a; Fan et al., 2026). Most closely related,
MiraBench shows that visually plausible predictions may ignore failure-inducing perturbations or
exhibit optimism bias (Yang et al., 2026a). Beyond image-space motion, recovered-action execu-
tion, and task-level failure judgments, WorldEcho aligns generated end-effector trajectories with
simulator-replayed references in SE(3) across broader numerical action queries.

3.3.1         ACTION Q UERY C ONSTRUCTION
Demonstrated Action replays the expert sequence for the current observation as an in-distribution
baseline. Four off-expert categories probe beyond this support. Cross-State Replay transfers expert
actions to another initial state, testing context-dependent effects including failure. Local Pertur-
bation applies bounded changes to expert actions to test sensitivity to nearby alternatives. Policy
Rollout uses learned-policy actions to capture deviations during evaluation and improvement. Be-
yond these policy-dependent queries, Feasible-Space Sampling draws valid actions from the broader
action space to test controllability. All off-expert queries undergo feasibility filtering before replay
in RoboTwin.

3.3.2         V ISUAL I NTEGRITY A SSESSMENT
We assess four aspects of visual integrity so trajectory comparisons concern valid depictions of the
robot and scene. Following WorldArena (Shang et al., 2026a), image quality q uses MUSIQ (Ke
et al., 2021), while motion smoothness m uses frame-interpolation consistency (Zhang et al., 2024).
With I denoting an indicator, thresholds τq , τm give Gquality = I[q ≥ τq ] and Gmotion = I[m ≥ τm ].
For end-effector visibility, GEEF passes when either SAM-based video tracking or framewise text-
prompted detection meets its requirement (Carion et al., 2026). Arm integrity Garm is assessed by
a vision-language evaluator that detects blurred, broken, or disappearing arms (Bai et al., 2025).
Using the same rules and thresholds for all models, we define
                                                        Gvis = Gquality ∧ Gmotion ∧ GEEF ∧ Garm .                                                                  (2)

3.4.1           ACTION C OVERAGE E XPANSION
We broaden expert-only training with multi-task simulated expert trajectories, local perturbations,
cross-state replays, policy rollouts, and broad feasible actions. We mix in a small set of target-task
real-robot demonstrations to preserve target-domain visual fidelity. Across simulated and real-robot
data, actions use relative Cartesian end-effector pose displacements in the robot base frame and
gripper opening. This shared representation allows the model to learn relationships between actions
and their consequences across the two domains. Appendix E.2 examines the effect of training-data
composition.

3.4.2           ACTION -F ORCING E XPERT
Broader coverage alone does not ensure that intermediate video representations encode action-
induced robot dynamics. To provide an auxiliary feature-level grounding signal, AFE maintains
one learnable trajectory query per future step. These queries progressively cross-attend to the inter-
mediate features of successive video blocks and predict future end-effector state changes relative to
the initial state. The targets include both arms’ position, rotation, and gripper state. Given standard-
ized cumulative-change targets y1:H and predictions ŷ1:H , we optimize
                                                                      LAFE = MSEvalid (ŷ1:H , y1:H ),                                                                                           (8)
where the masked mean squared error uses valid state labels. Appendix E.1 specifies target prepro-
cessing and design choices. AFE neither reads actions directly nor writes back to the video stream;
its loss updates the backbone through video features, and the expert is removed at inference time.

4.1      E XPERIMENTAL S ETUP

Benchmarks and evaluation sets. We evaluate action following on 50 RoboTwin tasks (Mu et al.,
2025; Chen et al., 2026b) using 1,750 queries from WorldEcho’s five categories, and component
contributions on ten tasks under the same protocol. Policy improvement is evaluated in RoboTwin
and on four real-robot tasks across three platforms (Appendix A).

Baselines. We compare WorldSync with CtrlWorld (Guo et al., 2026b), Cosmos-
Predict2.5 (NVIDIA et al., 2025), Cosmos3 (NVIDIA, 2026), DreamDojo (Gao et al., 2026),
Motus (Bi et al., 2025), and LingBotVA (Li et al., 2026a). Each baseline is trained with Expert
Demonstrations or Expanded Action Coverage and evaluated on the same task split and queries. All
baselines and both WorldSync variants are evaluated after 40k fine-tuning updates.

Metrics. Integrity-gated error is the primary metric. Raw pose-aware NDTW and visual pass rate
distinguish action mismatch from visual failure. Raw and gated errors are task-macro averages.
Visual pass rate is the fraction of accepted rollouts. Table 1 specifies video-metric aggregation.

4.3   P OLICY I MPROVEMENT

We adapt the VLAW framework (Guo et al., 2026a) to compare WorldSync with expert-trained Ctrl-
World under a shared policy-improvement procedure. Both conditions use the same initial policy
and matched interaction, world-model rollout, and policy-training budgets within each task. Ap-
pendix C details the simulation procedure and condition-specific world-model adaptation.
Simulation. On Bin Dumping, both conditions started at 52% success. WorldSync reached 65%
versus 57% for CtrlWorld after two rounds (Figure 6a). From 3,000 imagined rollouts per round,
the same task-progress and action-consistency filters retained 553 versus 452 rollouts in Round 1
and 365 versus 323 in Round 2 (Appendix C.3). Thus, WorldSync provided 22.3% and 13.0% more
usable imagined rollouts under the same generation budget, alongside larger policy gains.

5   C ONCLUSION AND L IMITATIONS
In this work, we introduced WorldEcho to evaluate action-conditioned world models beyond the
narrow distribution of expert demonstrations, jointly measuring visual integrity and action-induced
trajectory alignment. Our evaluation shows that the six expert-trained models degrade under feasible
off-expert actions, exposing failures overlooked by expert-only protocols. Guided by this diagno-
sis, we proposed WorldSync, which combines distributional coverage, representational grounding,
and intervention-effect alignment for faithful action-conditioned generation. WorldSync reduced
integrity-gated error on RoboTwin and yielded larger policy gains than expert-trained CtrlWorld
in simulation and on real robots. Although WorldEcho substantially broadens evaluation coverage,
comprehensively probing long-horizon interactions across diverse embodiments and open-world en-
vironments remains a shared challenge for the field and an important direction for future work.

Action Space. Both datasets use a unified 20-dimensional action representation with ten dimen-
sions per arm: three for translation increments, six for relative rotation, and one for gripper opening
at the next timestep. Pose increments are computed between adjacent observed states in the robot
base frame. Given successive end-effector rotations Rt and Rt+1 , we form the relative rotation
∆Rt = Rt+1 Rt⊤ . We use a 6D rotation representation by concatenating the first two columns of
∆Rt . We apply min–max normalization only to translation. The six rotation components are already
bounded in [−1, 1]; we retain their original values to preserve the unit-length, orthogonal column
structure without dataset-dependent rescaling. Gripper opening ranges from zero (closed) to one
(open).
We adapt this representation to each dataset. DROID’s single-arm actions use the ten components
allocated to the right arm. The unused left-arm components encode zero translation, identity rota-
tion, and a zero gripper value. For AgiBot dexterous-hand episodes, we retain end-effector motion
and set the gripper components to zero without encoding individual finger motion.

Video and Sampling. Each training clip contains one current frame, 32 future frames, and 32
actions. All three views are resized to 320 × 256 pixels. AgiBot uses head and wrist views, while
DROID’s two external cameras and wrist camera are assigned to the three view inputs in that or-
der. Language conditions use episode descriptions; the 18,863 retained DROID episodes without
descriptions use an empty string.
Clips are sampled with a stride of 32, with a final overlapping clip covering each episode’s tail. All
clips stay within episode boundaries and contain real future frames. This yields 1,741,651 AgiBot
clips and 884,670 DROID clips. We shuffle and traverse these clips without replacement, giving
each dataset a sampling share proportional to its clip count.

Training Configuration. We initialize from the official Wan2.2-TI2V-5B checkpoint (Team Wan
et al., 2025) and train the video diffusion transformer (DiT) and Action AdaLN modules described
in Appendix D, using the future-video flow-matching objective, keeping the VAE and text encoder
frozen. AFE and IE are disabled during pretraining. We train for one epoch on 16 NVIDIA A100
GPUs over 10 days, accumulating gradients over four microbatches per optimizer update for a global
batch size of 128. Training uses bfloat16 precision, ZeRO stage 2, and gradient checkpointing.
We use AdamW with a learning rate of 10−5 , 500 warmup steps, and cosine decay to 5 × 10−7 .
The optimizer uses (β1 , β2 ) = (0.9, 0.95), ϵ = 10−8 , weight decay 10−8 , and gradient clipping at a
norm of 1.0. The random seed is 45. Model weights are saved every 5,000 updates and at the end of
pretraining.

C     P OLICY I MPROVEMENT A PPROACH
Following the diagnose–improve–validate workflow in the Introduction, we evaluate world-model
quality through downstream policy improvement. While WorldEcho directly measures visual in-
tegrity and action following, policy success after post-training assesses the value of generated expe-
rience for learning to complete a task. We build on VLAW (Guo et al., 2026a) to compare world
models through a common policy-improvement procedure. §C.1 examines failures in our repro-
duction and explains the resulting changes to the pipeline. §C.2 describes the shared experimental
protocol and implementation, and §C.3 reports rollout retention and policy success over two im-
provement rounds.

C.1   D IAGNOSIS AND A DAPTATIONS

Reproduction and diagnosis. VLAW (Guo et al., 2026a) alternates world-model adaptation and
policy improvement using online and imagined experience. In our reproduction with CtrlWorld and
Qwen-OFT on RoboTwin’s rotate_qrcode task, policy success decreased from 59% to 46% and
then 14% (Table 4). This decline motivates examining whether imagined rollouts provide reliable
observation–action supervision. We use this reproduction to diagnose failures before comparing
world models under the adapted pipeline on the bin-dumping task below.

Two weaknesses undermine this supervision. First, generated rollouts may fail to advance the task
or depict apparent progress through visual artifacts, such as distorted arms or disappearing grippers
(Figure 2). Second, visually plausible motion may not follow the conditioning actions. Figure 18
shows a bin-dumping example: the policy’s actions fail to pick up the cup and empty its contents
in the simulator, yet the world model predicts successful lifting and pouring. This mismatch is
consistent with a bias toward successful expert behavior when responding to off-expert actions.
This discrepancy motivates screening both predicted task progress and action consistency before
using imagined rollouts for policy training.

Adaptations to VLAW. We retain VLAW’s iterative organization of online collection, world-
model adaptation, closed-loop imagination, and supervised policy updates. Within this organization,
we refine how imagined experience is selected and adapt the policy update to Qwen-OFT. We use
Robo-Dopamine (Tan et al., 2026b) as the task-progress reward model R to remove rollouts with

insufficient predicted progress. Because apparent progress can coexist with action mismatch, action-
consistency filtering then combines video–action rotation agreement with a paired-expert reference
feature, using AnyPos (Tan et al., 2026a) as the pose extractor Φ. The first stage selects predicted
progress, while the second checks the associated motion. Neither score replaces the simulator’s
success label.

D     A NALYSIS OF ACTION -C ONDITIONING M ECHANISMS
D.1   ACTION -C ONDITIONING M ECHANISMS

Action conditioning is central to robot world models because the model must translate commanded
robot motion into its visual consequences. For video-DiT-based world models, this requirement
raises two coupled design questions of how actions should be represented and where they should
interact with video features. We therefore systematically compare nine mechanisms selected from
representative works and reimplemented within the same backbone, organizing them along four
design axes of action representation, temporal or spatial alignment, injection site, and fusion. Table 8
defines these mechanisms, while Figure 19 illustrates their corresponding data flows through the DiT
backbone.
Six numerical-based mechanisms retain actions as low-dimensional signals. Additive follows
NanoWM (Huang et al., 2026a) and adds frame-aligned action features directly to the patch fea-
tures. AdaLN Fusion keeps the same frame alignment but routes actions through the native AdaLN
path, where the action embedding is fused into the timestep pathway that drives the backbone’s
adaptive layer normalization, as in Cosmos-Predict2.5 (NVIDIA et al., 2025) and DreamDojo (Gao
et al., 2026). Action AdaLN then moves this modulation from this shared conditioning path into
each block; following IRASim (Zhu et al., 2025), it produces low-rank shift, scale, and gate offsets

action-induced changes should appear. Holding the action-map representation fixed further isolates
the effect of their injection paths.

D.4                  C OMPUTATIONAL C OST

The nine mechanisms introduce overhead through three distinct profiles (Table 9). The additive,
modulation, and attention variants are parameter-dominated, with Additive, AdaLN Fusion, Action
AdaLN, Cross-Attention, and Shared-KV Attention each adding 566.49–572.01M trainable param-
eters but little additional inference compute (0.04–4.93 TFLOPs). The sequence-extending variants
are compute-dominated. Latent Frame and Sequence Concatenation add no trainable parameters,
yet their additional latent tokens are processed by the full backbone at every denoising step, costing
69.39 and 520.90 TFLOPs, respectively. Channel Concatenation is nearly free, with 0.04M param-
eters and less than 0.01 TFLOPs, but has higher trajectory error than Action AdaLN and Latent
Frame. Parameter count and inference compute are therefore decoupled, since a mechanism can be
parameter-free yet expensive per rollout or parameter-heavy yet cheap to run. Neither number alone
summarizes the cost of a design.

D.5                        D ISCUSSION AND D ESIGN C HOICE

Two conclusions emerge from this study. First, no action-conditioning mechanism resolves action
following on its own. Across feature addition, adaptive modulation, attention-based fusion, latent-
frame conditioning, and rendered action maps, every variant still trades trajectory agreement against
visual integrity to some degree, and every variant adds parameters, inference compute, or both.
Improving the injection pathway shifts the operating point but does not close the gap. Our full
model therefore pairs its injection mechanism with broader action coverage and auxiliary training
objectives rather than relying on injection design alone.
Second, Action AdaLN achieves the lowest errors with a simple modulation design. It modulates
the attention and feed-forward sublayers of each DiT block through low-rank shift, scale, and gate
offsets, without extra tokens, a separate attention branch, or rendered maps. Yet this standard mod-
ulation design attains the lowest raw NDTW and integrity-gated error in Table 9, retains a 98.57%
visual pass rate, and adds only 0.09 TFLOPs of inference compute. The more elaborate alternatives,
including extra latent slots, joint action–video sequences, ControlNet-style residual branches, and
explicit spatial maps, fail to surpass this strong baseline on either error metric.
We therefore adopt Action AdaLN in our full model. This choice should not be read as uniform
superiority: Shared-KV Attention and Channel Concatenation match its visual pass rate, while La-
tent Frame adds no trainable parameters. What Action AdaLN offers is the most favorable balance
of accurate action following, visual integrity, inference compute, and architectural simplicity among
the nine candidates, which is the combination that matters for a general-purpose robot world model.

Motivation and design space. Intervention-Effect (IE) supervision compares generated futures
under paired actions. AFE adds a complementary constraint within each rollout: intermediate video
features should retain the robot-state changes induced by the actions. This training-only objective
raises three design questions. Where to read? Features at different backbone depths may differ in
their usefulness for state prediction. When to read? Restricting a future-step query to its correspond-
ing video time may focus supervision but remove useful context. What to predict? Absolute state,
stepwise change, and initial-relative cumulative change emphasize different aspects of robot motion.
We compare these choices to select the AFE supervision.

Design choices. AFE architecture. Figure 23(a) shows the two input paths. Actions condition
the Wan video backbone; AFE reads its intermediate video-token features. Ground-truth robot states
enter only the supervision path, where they define targets for the auxiliary loss. They are not inputs
to AFE. Wan-VAE compresses the current frame and 32 future frames into nine latent time positions.
The backbone processes these noisy latents with visual, language, and action conditioning.
AFE maintains H = 32 learnable queries qh , one per future step, arranged as Q0 = [q1 ; . . . ; qH ] ∈
RH×d , where d is the query feature dimension. The (i + 1)-th of eight sequential blocks reads the
zero-based backbone layer ℓi = 8 + 2i and updates Qi to Qi+1 , for 0 ≤ i < 8. As shown in
Figure 23(b), each block uses global query self-attention, video-feature cross-attention, and a feed-
forward network, with pre-normalization and residual connections. A shared LayerNorm–linear
projection maps the backbone features into the AFE feature space.

F.2              M ETRIC D ISTRIBUTIONS AND A NALYSIS

To support policy evaluation, world-model metrics must distinguish differences in visual integrity
and action following. Blurred imagery, distorted robot structures, and motion inconsistent with the
commanded actions can each make generated rollouts unreliable for policy learning. We there-
fore examine whether WorldEcho scores and gate decisions correspond to observable differences
in generated videos. We combine quantitative score distributions and per-check pass/fail rates with
qualitative examples across score ranges to connect the metric values to visual integrity and action
following.

F.3   AGREEMENT WITH H UMAN E VALUATION

We examined how WorldEcho’s automatic measurements correspond to human judgments, com-
plementing the metric distributions and examples in Appendix F.2. We first assess whether raw
NDTW captures human judgments of action following, then examine the four visual checks used
for preliminary screening.

Evaluation protocol. Eleven raters provided 544 ratings for 100 clips, with five or six ratings per
clip. Forty action clips received ratings on a five-point scale, with higher scores indicating greater
action agreement. The remaining 60 clips received binary pass/fail judgments, with 15 clips each
for image quality, motion smoothness, EEF visibility, and arm integrity. We retained all observed
ratings, using the median for each action clip and a strict majority for each visual clip. No visual
clip had a tied vote.

Sampling and analysis. The action set covers four tasks and five action-query cate-
gories, with two clips per combination. The tasks are dump_bin_bigbin, lift_pot,
place_burger_fries, and rotate_qrcode. For action following, we computed Spear-
man’s correlation between raw NDTW and median human ratings. A 95% percentile interval was
obtained from 10,000 bootstrap resamples of clips within each task, retaining each clip’s observed
ratings. For visual metrics, we compared the recorded automatic decisions with human majority la-
bels using accuracy, pass recall, failure recall, and Cohen’s κ. Krippendorff’s α measures inter-rater
agreement, using ordinal distances for action scores and nominal distances for visual labels.

Action-following metric. Raw NDTW showed a strong negative association with median human
action ratings (Figure 30(a)). Spearman’s ρ was −0.826, with a 95% CI of [−0.911, −0.679], in-
dicating that lower trajectory errors corresponded to higher perceived action agreement. This result
supports trajectory alignment as the central measure of action following in WorldEcho across the
sampled tasks and action-query categories. Ordinal Krippendorff’s α was 0.594, so the correlation
reflects agreement with aggregated human judgments despite variation among individual raters.

Visual metrics. The visual checks are intended to screen for basic visual integrity while preserv-
ing clips for action-following evaluation. Automatic decisions matched human majority labels on
80.0%, 80.0%, 93.3%, and 86.7% of image-quality, motion-smoothness, EEF-visibility, and arm-
integrity clips, respectively (Figure 30(b)). Each check retained 90.0%–100.0% of human-passed
clips, while rejecting 50.0%–80.0% of human-failed clips (Table 12). This asymmetry is consistent
with the intended permissive screening, which limits visual rejection so that retained clips can be
evaluated for action following. For clips passing all four checks, raw NDTW determines the official
error, keeping trajectory agreement central to the evaluation. Some human-rated visual failures nev-
ertheless passed the automatic checks, so this screening does not guarantee visual integrity. These
per-check results describe 15 sampled clips per dimension.

Figures 31 and 32 compare WorldSync with six baseline models on simulation tasks from WorldE-
cho. For each comparison, both models receive the same initial observation and action sequence,
with the simulator replay providing ground truth. Rows show ground truth, WorldSync, and the cor-
responding baseline at matched frame indices. The final column enlarges the marked region using
the same relative image coordinates across rows, highlighting local pose and shape differences. Fig-
ure 33 reports the corresponding WorldEcho metrics, computed on the complete generated videos
rather than the displayed frames or enlarged regions.
Figure 31 shows differences in arm structure and gripper motion. In Move playing card, CtrlWorld
produces distorted arm and gripper shapes. WorldSync better preserves their structure and follows
the downward movement in the ground-truth replay. In Move pill bottle, Cosmos-Predict2.5 largely
retains the initial right-gripper rotation, whereas WorldSync captures part of the wrist rotation. In
Place dual shoes, Cosmos3 moves the grippers toward the shoe box. WorldSync keeps them to its
sides, more closely matching the ground-truth replay.
Figure 32 shows differences in wrist motion and arm placement. In Stack blocks (three), DreamDojo
largely preserves the initial left-wrist pose. WorldSync follows the direction of the wrist motion,
although its amplitude remains smaller than in the ground-truth replay. In Grab roller, Motus retracts
the extended arm, while WorldSync more closely maintains its ground-truth placement. In Place
bread basket, LingBotVA moves the grippers toward the basket, whereas WorldSync better preserves
their placement relative to ground truth.
The per-case metrics in Figure 33 distinguish visual validity from motion agreement. WorldSync
passes all four visual checks in all six cases and has lower raw NDTW in each comparison. Ctrl-
World, Motus, and LingBotVA fail the end-effector visibility check; Motus also fails arm integrity.
Their integrity-gated errors therefore take the fixed value of 0.3. Cosmos-Predict2.5, Cosmos3, and
DreamDojo pass all visual checks, but their raw NDTW errors remain higher than WorldSync’s
(0.0714 versus 0.0251, 0.2532 versus 0.0256, and 0.0604 versus 0.0224, respectively). These cases
show that passing the visual gate does not ensure agreement with the conditioned motion.
The individual scores also expose differences that the overall gate does not rank. For example,
DreamDojo has higher image quality than WorldSync (0.679 versus 0.484), despite its larger trajec-
tory error. Likewise, a passing arm-integrity decision does not rule out local shape distortions, as
the CtrlWorld example illustrates. The visualizations and per-case metrics therefore provide com-
plementary evidence.

differences in wrist rotation and object appearance. They were chosen after assessing trajectory
error, visual integrity, and the complete generated sequences. The comparisons illustrate specific
visual discrepancies; aggregate performance over the full evaluation set is reported in Table 1.
